How it works

1 · Where am I? (GPS)

Your phone hears signals from satellites. Each signal says how far away that satellite is, so the phone sits somewhere on a circle around it. Three circles meet at one point: that's you. Drag the satellites.

2 · Phones send their speed

Phones with location turned on send anonymous pings: which road, and how fast. Here the pings are fake. A jam sends slow pings. The 99-phone wagon sends 99 pings at walking pace, like Simon Weckert's 2020 stunt in Berlin.

3 · The live speed of each road

Average all the pings on a road. A few fast cars can't beat 99 slow phones, so the wagon makes an empty road look jammed.

4 · Green, orange, red

Compare the live speed with the speed the road has when it's empty. Under 75% turns orange. Under 40% turns red.

5 · Predicting your arrival time

A jam now may be gone when you get there. The prediction slides today's live speed back to the usual speed as the minutes pass. Move the fade slider and watch the fastest route change.

6 · The best route

Dijkstra's algorithm grows outward from the start, always taking the cheapest junction next. It runs twice: once with kilometres as the cost (shortest), once with predicted minutes (fastest). Plain Dijkstra is fine for 2 km. Real engines use Contraction Hierarchies or multi-level techniques to search whole continents in milliseconds.

The routing algorithm: Dijkstra on a road graph

  1. 1 · Roads become nodes and edges

    The map is about 2 km of OpenStreetMap roads: roads, each a line of GPS points. Every point becomes a node. A point shared by two roads is a junction ( of them). Each pair of neighbouring points becomes an edge in both directions, priced by its real length (haversine distance). Total: nodes and edges.

  2. 2 · Clean the graph, snap A and B

    Roads cut by the edge of the box leave small islands. A flood fill keeps the biggest connected piece ( nodes, dropped), so every pair of points can be routed. When you pick A or B, the pin snaps to the nearest node on that piece.

    Now: A snaps to node , m from your pick. B snaps to node , m away.

  3. 3 · Price each edge

    The same search runs twice with a different cost per edge:

    • Shortest: cost = edge length in km.
    • Fastest: cost = km ÷ predicted speed × 60, in minutes. The predicted speed starts at the live speed (average of phone pings) and fades back to the usual speed as the minutes you have already driven grow.

    Now: phone pings, jammed roads, fade min. Cheapest total cost: km (shortest), min (fastest).

  4. 4 · Dijkstra finds the cheapest path

    1. Give A a cost of 0. Every other node is unknown.
    2. Take the unvisited node with the lowest cost so far.
    3. For each edge out of it, compute cost so far + edge cost. Keep it if it beats the neighbour's best.
    4. Remember which edge gave each best cost.
    5. Stop when B is taken. Walk the remembered edges back from B to A to get the route.

    Dijkstra is exact because every edge cost is positive: once a node is taken, no later path can be cheaper.

Live calculation for the

Each row is one road on the route, split into its node-to-node edges. OpenStreetMap stores long streets in pieces, so a name can repeat. The white dots on the map are the nodes. Minutes add up edge by edge, so each road's predicted speed depends on when you reach it.

Simplifications: every road is two-way (one-way tags are ignored). The frontier is a plain list scan, not a heap, which is fine for 2,600 nodes. Real engines like Google Maps use Contraction Hierarchies or multi-level graphs to search whole continents in milliseconds.
